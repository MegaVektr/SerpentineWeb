NIS2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) is one of those regulations that
generates more guidance than the text itself. Most of the guidance is
not wrong. Much of it leaves out the parts that matter operationally.
This post is a plain-language reading of NIS2 obligations for SaaS
providers — what is actually written, what is enforceable, and what
the supervisory authorities are likely to look for.

We are not lawyers. This is engineering and compliance reading, not
legal advice. Talk to counsel for the formal interpretation in your
jurisdiction.

## Are you in scope, and as what

NIS2 distinguishes **essential entities** (Annex I) from **important
entities** (Annex II). The classification matters because supervisory
regimes and penalty ceilings differ.

For SaaS providers, the relevant categories most often live in
Annex I sector 8 (digital infrastructure) and Annex II sector 6
(digital providers). A cloud computing service provider, a managed
service provider, a managed security service provider, an online
marketplace, a search engine provider, and a social networking
service all sit in this region.

The size threshold is the medium-size enterprise definition under
EU Recommendation 2003/361/EC: at least 50 employees or annual
turnover above €10 million. There are exceptions where smaller
entities are still in scope — DNS service providers, top-level
domain name registries, and certain trust service providers are
covered regardless of size.

If your SaaS sells to entities that are themselves in scope, you may
also be pulled in via supply chain obligations even if you are not
directly classified. We come back to that below.

## Article 21: the ten measure categories

Article 21(2) lists ten categories of measures that in-scope
entities must take. It is the operational core of the directive.

1. **Policies on risk analysis and information system security.**
2. **Incident handling.**
3. **Business continuity** — backups, recovery, crisis management.
4. **Supply chain security**, including the security of relationships
   with direct suppliers.
5. **Security in network and information systems acquisition,
   development, and maintenance**, including vulnerability handling
   and disclosure.
6. **Policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of
   cybersecurity risk-management measures.**
7. **Basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training.**
8. **Policies and procedures regarding the use of cryptography and,
   where appropriate, encryption.**
9. **Human resources security, access control policies, and asset
   management.**
10. **Multi-factor authentication or continuous authentication
    solutions, secured voice/video/text communications, and secured
    emergency communication systems.**

The directive uses the phrase "appropriate and proportionate." That
is not a loophole. Supervisory authorities can and will ask why a
measure was omitted, and the answer must be defensible against the
entity's risk assessment. "We did a risk assessment and decided this
was not material" is a valid answer if the risk assessment is real.
"We did not have a risk assessment" is not.

## Article 23: incident reporting

Article 23 sets a three-stage reporting timeline for significant
incidents.

**Within 24 hours** of becoming aware of a significant incident, an
**early warning** must be submitted to the relevant CSIRT or
competent authority. The early warning must indicate whether the
incident is suspected to be caused by unlawful or malicious acts and
whether it could have a cross-border impact.

**Within 72 hours** of awareness, an **incident notification** must
follow, including an initial assessment, severity, impact, and
indicators of compromise.

**Within one month** of the incident notification, a **final report**
must be submitted, including a detailed description, the type of
threat or root cause, applied and ongoing mitigation, and any
cross-border impact.

These deadlines run from awareness, not from incident occurrence.
Supervisory authorities are explicit that an entity cannot avoid the
clock by being slow to investigate.

A "significant incident" is defined functionally: it has caused or is
capable of causing severe operational disruption or financial loss,
or has affected or is capable of affecting other natural or legal
persons through considerable material or non-material damage. The
threshold is meaningfully lower than legacy frameworks.

## Supply chain — Article 21(2)(d)

Article 21(2)(d) requires entities to consider vulnerabilities
specific to each direct supplier and the overall quality of products
and cybersecurity practices of their suppliers. Recital 90 makes
clear that this extends to ICT service providers and to the entity's
critical suppliers in the cybersecurity domain.

Operationally, this means three things for SaaS providers:

- A supplier register exists, with classification by criticality.
- Each critical supplier has a documented assessment of its security
  practices.
- Contracts with critical suppliers include security requirements
  that are enforceable, not aspirational.

If you sell into NIS2 entities, expect to be asked for evidence
against this from your customers. The questions are increasingly
specific.

## Article 20: governance and board accountability

Article 20 makes the management body of an entity responsible for
approving, overseeing, and being trained in cybersecurity risk
management. Article 20(2) is explicit: members of management bodies
must follow training, and entities must offer similar training to
their employees on a regular basis.

This is a structural change from earlier frameworks. The board cannot
delegate the obligation to the CISO. The board can be held personally
liable for non-compliance, including monetary fines and, in some
member-state implementations, temporary prohibitions on holding
managerial functions.

For SaaS, the practical implication is that board minutes need to
show cybersecurity risk has been substantively reviewed — not just
filed.

## What is actually enforceable

NIS2 is a directive, transposed by each member state into national
law, with member-state-specific supervisory authorities and
penalties. The directive sets ceilings: for essential entities, up to
€10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover; for important
entities, up to €7 million or 1.4%.

Enforcement priorities vary by member state but cluster around the
same themes: incident reporting timelines, supply chain
documentation, board-level governance, and Article 21 measures with
demonstrable evidence.

If you take one thing from NIS2: the directive is evidence-driven,
not policy-driven. Policies that are not enforced, measures that are
not implemented, and reports that arrive on day five rather than day
one are the failure modes that supervisory authorities are most
likely to examine. Preparing for NIS2 is, in practice, preparing to
produce evidence on demand.
