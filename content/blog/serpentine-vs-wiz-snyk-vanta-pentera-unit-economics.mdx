The standard mid-market security stack has settled into a recognizable
shape: a cloud posture tool, an application security tool, a GRC tool,
an offensive testing tool, and some glue. Each tool is competent in
its lane. Together they produce a renewal cost that grows roughly
linearly with the number of tools, and an integration cost that grows
faster than that.

This post compares Serpentine to a representative version of that
stack and looks at where the unit economics actually come from. We
are not arguing that the point tools are bad products — many of them
are excellent in their domain. We are arguing that the architecture of
five tools is not the architecture of one platform, and that the
difference shows up in the bill.

## What the comparison is and is not

We are comparing architectures, not feature lists. A feature-list
comparison would pit a cloud posture module against a dedicated CSPM
vendor, an application security module against a dedicated AppSec
vendor, and so on. In a feature-list cell-by-cell comparison, the
specialist usually wins.

The architectural comparison asks a different question: what does it
cost, in money and in human work, to operate the *combined* stack
over a contract cycle? That is the question customers actually answer
at renewal time, and the answer is dominated by integration costs, not
feature cells.

## Where the integrated stack wins on cost

Three forces compound in favor of an integrated platform.

**Evidence reuse.** A configuration snapshot collected by Postava is
the same snapshot Regulativa cites for a control, and the same
snapshot Odbrana attaches to a related finding. In a five-tool stack,
the equivalent snapshot is collected three times — once by the
posture tool, once by the GRC tool, once by the offensive tool —
because none of them trust the others' representation of the world.
Each collection has a cost: API calls, agent overhead, customer time,
review effort.

**Schema reconciliation.** Five tools produce five schemas. The work
of mapping them into a coherent picture lives somewhere — usually
inside the customer, often inside a security engineer's head. We have
seen organizations where two FTEs effectively do nothing but
reconcile asset inventories across tools. That cost does not show up
on any vendor's invoice but it is a real recurring expense.

**Procurement and renewal overhead.** Each tool has its own contract,
SOC 2 review, DPA, pricing model, sales cycle, and renewal
negotiation. None of these scale. Procurement teams quietly absorb the
work, then pass it back to security teams as deadline pressure.

A unified platform does not eliminate these costs — it does not need
to — but it absorbs most of them into one schema, one contract, one
collection layer. The customer's marginal cost per added scope is
small.

## Where point tools win

We should be honest about the other side.

**Depth in a single domain.** A focused CSPM vendor with a hundred
engineers on cloud posture will have detection coverage that no
generalist platform can match in that one slice. Customers whose
threat model is dominated by cloud misconfiguration are correct to
weight that depth heavily.

**Speed to a new ecosystem.** When a new cloud, language, or
framework appears, a focused vendor will often ship support for it
faster than an integrated platform that has to fit the new ecosystem
into its canonical model.

**Independence from a single vendor.** Some buyers genuinely value
the ability to swap one component without disrupting the others. A
unified platform is a stronger commitment.

These trade-offs are real, and we do not pretend otherwise.

## What the comparison looks like in practice

Consider four functions: cloud posture, code/application security,
GRC, and offensive testing. In a point-tool stack, these are four
contracts, four schemas, four control mappings, four sets of
credentials, four sets of evidence collection, four renewal
conversations.

In Serpentine, they are four product surfaces — Postava, Odbrana,
Regulativa, Napad — over one canonical model. The same asset record
is read by all four. The same control mapping is referenced by all
four. The same evidence record can satisfy claims in any of the four.

The savings are not magic. They come from the fact that work done
once does not have to be done three more times in slightly different
shapes.

## What this means at renewal

If you are evaluating Serpentine against a stack of point tools, the
honest comparison has three columns: per-tool licence cost,
integration cost (often hidden), and depth-in-domain cost (where
specialists genuinely lead). Serpentine should win on the first two
and concede on the third in any single dimension.

We think that is the right trade for most buyers most of the time.
For organizations whose threat model genuinely requires best-in-class
depth in one specific area, a point tool is still defensible. The
worst answer is the unconsidered five-tool stack, where every line
item was best-in-class on the day it was bought, and the integration
work has been someone's full-time job ever since.

The renewal conversation is the right place to do this math. We
encourage customers to do it in front of us. The architecture should
hold up under that scrutiny, and we think it does.
